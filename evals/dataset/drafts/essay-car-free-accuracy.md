Whenever a city announces, that its centre will be closed to private cars, the
argument what follows is louder than the traffic ever was. Supporters and
opponents both have a case and it is worth to set the two side by side, what the
policy does to the people who live there, and to the shops which serve them.

For residents the gains are immediate and phisical. Air that was measurably
harmful becomes breathable, a street that was a corridor becomes a place where
children can be let out of sight for a moment, the noise that made an open
window impossible in July simply stops. Against that stands a real loss of
comfort. An elderly resident, who cannot walk to a tram stop, or a parent
carrying a week of shopping up three flights of stairs, gets a daily problem in
exchange for a general good.

Shopkeepers protest usually first and benefit most. Their fear is reasonable,
customers who arrive by car spend more per visit and a builder's merchant cannot
deliver bathroom tiles by bicycle. Actually the evidence from cities that have
already done it points the other way. Streets without cars attract people who
stay, and a customer who walks past a window twice a day comes eventually in.

Therefore works the policy, but only when the alternatives arrive first. Close a
centre while the trams are still infrequent and the loading rules unwritten and
the opposition will be right for reasons entirely avoidable.
