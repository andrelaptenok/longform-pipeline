Every September another school is announcing that its pupils will carry a tablet
instead of bag full of books. The decision has convinced supporters and
determined opponents alike, and both can point on consequences worth weighing:
what it does with learning, and what it costs.

The first question is what happens with learning itself. Tablet puts an entire
library into pupil's hands: difficult passage can be looked up, listened or
translated in seconds, and teacher can return a corrected essay the same evening
rather than week later. The same device, however, is a doorway to everything
else. A pupil who is reading three pages and then drifts into group chat has not
read three pages at all, and paper, whatever else may be said against it, never
offered this temptation.

The second question is money, and here the picture is less encouraging.
Textbooks are bought once and passed down since years, while tablets are
replaced every few years, break when dropped, and demand a network which many
buildings still are lacking. Family which cannot afford a repair is quietly left
behind, what is not a difficulty a printed book ever created.

I am therefore believing that tablets belong to schools as an addition rather
than replacement. Where the equipment is funded and rules for using it are
clear, pupils are gaining a great deal. Where it arrives simply because it looks
modern, they lose twice over: once to distraction, and once to cost.
