# The hours nobody pays for

Ask a group of eighteen-year-olds what they did last weekend and almost none of
them will mention shelter or food bank. Ask the same group if volunteering is
worthwhile and nearly all of them will say that it is. This gap is worth to
explain.

What volunteers gain is rarely what they are expecting to gain. A student who
spends two months in animal shelter learns to come on time, to work beside
people which she did not choose, and to finish a task that nobody will praise
her. Those habits are carrying over into everything else, and they say more
about person than any certificate collected on a weekend workshop. There is also
plainer reward of being needed, which on eighteen is harder to come by than
adults tend to remember.

Why then so few of them take part? Partly because school leaves little room:
matura year swallows evenings and weekends whole. Partly because the offer is
invisible, since organisations are advertising among people who already
volunteer, and teenager willing to help often has no idea whom to ask. And
partly because first step feels enormous, as walking alone into building full of
strangers usually does.

None of this is beyond solving. A noticeboard, a teacher willing to make one
telephone call, a friend who comes along on first day: this is usually whole
distance between wanting to help and helping.
