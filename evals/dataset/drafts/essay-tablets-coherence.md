Textbooks are bought once and passed down for years. Tablets are replaced every
few years, break when dropped, demand a network many buildings still lack. A
family that cannot afford a repair is quietly left behind. A printed book never
created that difficulty.

Tablets belong in schools as an addition rather than a replacement. Every
September another school announces that its pupils will carry a tablet instead
of a bag full of books. Supporters and determined opponents can point to
consequences worth weighing.

A pupil who reads three pages and then drifts into a group chat has not read
three pages at all. A tablet puts an entire library into a pupil's hands. A
difficult passage can be looked up, listened to or translated in seconds. The
device is a doorway to everything else. Paper never offered that temptation. A
teacher can return a corrected essay the same evening rather than a week later.

Where the equipment is funded and the rules for using it are clear, pupils gain
a great deal. The second question is money. Where a tablet arrives simply
because it looks modern, pupils lose twice over, to distraction and to cost. The
decision has convinced supporters and opponents alike, and what it does to
learning is worth weighing against what it costs.
