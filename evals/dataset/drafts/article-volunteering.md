# The hours nobody pays for

Ask a group of eighteen-year-olds what they did last weekend and almost none
will mention a shelter or a food bank. Ask the same group whether volunteering
is worthwhile and nearly all of them will say it is. That gap is worth
explaining.

What volunteers gain is rarely what they expect to gain. A student who spends
two months at an animal shelter learns to turn up on time, to work beside people
she did not choose, and to finish a task that nobody will praise her for.
Those habits carry over into everything else, and they say more about a person
than any certificate collected at a weekend workshop. There is also the plainer
reward of being needed, which at eighteen is harder to come by than adults tend
to remember.

Why, then, do so few take part? Partly because school leaves little room: a
matura year swallows evenings and weekends whole. Partly because the offer is
invisible, since organisations advertise among people who already volunteer,
and a teenager willing to help often has no idea whom to ask. And partly because
the first step feels enormous, as walking alone into a building full of
strangers usually does.

None of this is beyond solving. A noticeboard, a teacher willing to make one
telephone call, a friend who comes along on the first day: that is usually the
entire distance between wanting to help and helping.
