Whenever a city announces that its centre will be closed to private cars, the
argument that follows is louder than the traffic ever was. What matters most in
that argument is what the policy does to the people who actually live there.

For residents the gains are immediate and physical. Air that was measurably
harmful becomes breathable, a street that was a corridor becomes a place where
children can be let out of sight for a moment, and the noise that made an open
window impossible in July simply stops. Against that stands a real loss of
convenience. An elderly resident who cannot walk to a tram stop, or a parent
carrying a week of shopping up three flights of stairs, has been handed a daily
problem in exchange for a general good.

There is also the question of how a city looks to the rest of the world. A
centre photographed without a single parked car appears on posters and in travel
supplements, and the visitors who come because of them fill the squares all
summer. My own city put its market square on every brochure it printed the year
after the barriers went up, and the tourist office has not stopped mentioning it
since. A place that is pleasant to walk through is a place people describe to
their friends, which is worth more than any advertising campaign a council could
buy.

Closing a centre to cars is therefore right, provided the trams run often enough
for the people who live there.
